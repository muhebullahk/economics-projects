### Chapter 1: Beyond Rationality — The Foundations of Behavioural Economics

**Corresponds to:** Lecture 1 (Limits of Rationality)
**Textbook reference:** Angner, Chapters 1–2

**Topics covered:**
- The rational choice model: assumptions and predictions
- Expected Utility Theory (Von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944) and its four axioms
- The Allais Paradox and violations of the independence axiom
- The Ellsberg Paradox and ambiguity aversion
- Framing effects (the Asian Disease Problem)
- Loss aversion: the asymmetry between gains and losses
- Overconfidence and its economic consequences
- Present bias and time inconsistency
- Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon) and satisficing
- Dual-process theory: System 1 vs System 2
- Why markets do not automatically correct cognitive bias
- Economic costs of systematic bias
- Methods in behavioural economics
- History of the field and Nobel Prize contributions

**Learning objectives (10):** Define the rational agent model and EUT; state and interpret the four EUT axioms; describe Allais and Ellsberg paradoxes; explain framing effects, loss aversion, overconfidence, and present bias; define bounded rationality and satisficing; distinguish System 1 from System 2; evaluate market discipline as a corrective mechanism; assess economic costs of bias; identify research methods used in the field; situate behavioural economics in the history of economic thought.

**Key researchers:** Von Neumann, Morgenstern, Allais, Ellsberg, Kahneman, Tversky, Simon, Frederick, Thaler, Barber, Odean.

## Connections Between Chapters

The chapters in this textbook build on each other in the following ways:

**Chapters 1 → 2 → 3 (Foundations):**
Chapter 1 establishes the standard model and its failures. Chapter 2 explores the cognitive mechanisms (heuristics) that cause systematic errors. Chapter 3 presents the leading formal model (Prospect Theory) that replaces EUT and accounts for the findings in Chapters 1 and 2.

**Chapters 1, 3 → 4 → 5 (Intertemporal Choice):**
Present bias (introduced in Chapter 1) is formalised in Chapter 4, which develops the quasi-hyperbolic discounting model. Chapter 5 applies this framework to savings behaviour, the most economically important domain of intertemporal choice.

**Chapter 1 → 6 → 7 (Social and Strategic Behaviour):**
The assumption of pure self-interest (introduced in Chapter 1) is challenged in Chapter 6 by experimental evidence on social preferences. Chapter 7 shows how these social preferences alter the predictions of strategic interaction (game theory).

**Chapters 1–3 → 8 (Market Aggregation):**
Chapter 8 asks whether individual-level biases aggregate into market-level anomalies. It applies the findings of Chapters 1–3 to financial markets.

**Chapters 1–8 → 9 → 10 (Policy):**
Chapters 9 and 10 synthesise the insights from all previous chapters into a framework for behavioural policy design, addressing when and how policymakers should intervene to improve outcomes.

## Key Recurring Themes Across All Chapters

1. **The benchmark matters.** Behavioural departures are always measured relative to the standard rational model. Understanding what the standard model predicts is essential for understanding what the behavioural finding means.

2. **Errors are systematic, not random.** Behavioural economics studies predictable, replicable deviations from the standard model. This is what makes them economically significant and policy-relevant.

3. **Context shapes choice.** The environment in which decisions are made — the choice architecture — powerfully influences what people choose, often without their awareness.

4. **System 1 vs System 2.** Many of the patterns documented in this course reflect the operation of fast, automatic cognitive processes (System 1) in situations where slower, deliberate reasoning (System 2) would produce better outcomes.

5. **Policy implications follow from the model.** Different behavioural findings call for different policy responses. Understanding the mechanism is essential for designing effective interventions.
